## Before buying a new bag, look at what you already have

A change in pack quantity can send a purchasing team looking for a new bag size. Meanwhile, the warehouse may already hold several sizes for other products. If one of those bags can handle the new requirement, the business has an option it can evaluate before adding another line to its packaging catalogue.

Start with two questions. How many products fit in a bag you already stock? If several bags can hold the quantity you need, which one should you put on the line? The first question can help purchasing decide whether to look for another size. The second brings manufacturing and logistics into the decision. They need a pack that operators can fill and seal consistently and that fits the way the product is handled and shipped.

## How many toothpaste cartons fit in an existing bag?

Consider a boxed toothpaste carton measuring **45 × 35 × 190 mm** and weighing **150 g**. The warehouse has a bag with nominal flat dimensions of **350 × 200 mm**. For this example, KolliPack reserves **10 mm** for sealing and uses a **2 mm** fit tolerance to leave a little clearance when checking the fit.

The calculated capacity is **six cartons**. At that quantity, KolliPack reports **82% bag usage**. This compares the flat-bag area required by the arrangement with the nominal area of one face of the empty bag. It describes how the arrangement uses the stock bag’s flat dimensions. For more on how those dimensions relate to a three-dimensional pack, see the [flat-bag sizing article](/blog/flat-bag-sizing-soap-bar-arrangements/).

The six cartons weigh **900 g**. Adding about **3.93 g** of estimated bag film brings the packed weight to about **0.904 kg**. The estimate assumes a simple two-wall bag made from 1.2 mil low-density polyethylene (LDPE), about 0.03 mm thick.

If the order calls for six cartons per bag, purchasing can put the existing 350 × 200 mm bag on the shortlist before creating another packaging specification. The warehouse can check whether there is enough stock for the order, and the packing team knows which size to try first.

## Which available bag should hold six cereal bars?

This time the quantity is fixed. Each wrapped cereal bar measures **130 × 50 × 18 mm**, weighs **150 g**, and ships in a pack of **six bars**. The example catalogue contains eight stock sizes from **200 × 150 mm** to **400 × 280 mm**. The comparison uses 1.2 mil LDPE, a 10 mm sealing allowance and a 2 mm fit tolerance across the range.

All three bags in Figure 2 show six bars. The 220 × 160 mm bag reaches its calculated capacity at six. The 260 × 200 mm and 320 × 230 mm bags have calculated capacities of ten and twenty, with bag usage at those maximums of 86% and 91% respectively. For a six-bar order, the relevant figures are their usage percentages at six.

KolliPack ranks feasible catalogue bags by **highest bag usage for six bars**, using smaller flat-bag area to break a tie. The **220 × 160 mm** bag leads because it has the highest usage for the requested six bars, **87.27%**. The 300 × 200 mm bag ranks second, ahead of the smaller 240 × 180 mm bag. Checking the sizes in ascending order would miss that difference.

## What makes one stock bag the better business choice?

Start with the count the order requires and the bags your operation can reliably source. A size already used on another product may simplify purchasing and replenishment. If that stock is scarce, reserving it for a different job and using another feasible bag for this run may be the better decision.

For the same six bars and the same film specification, the smaller bag uses less film per pack and per bar. That gives a team a way to compare one part of the material footprint. The overall environmental and financial outcomes also depend on what happens on the line and during distribution, including how much packaging is discarded and how well the products are protected.

Ask operators how easily they can load and position the products for sealing. A bag with more room may be easier to handle or may serve both the six-pack and a larger pack size. It may also allow more movement inside the sealed bag. Check how the finished packs sit in their shipping cartons, whether they need extra protection, and how many fit per case. Those differences can affect the amount of secondary packaging and the space needed in storage and transport.

Compare the overall cost using the proposed order quantity and actual operating conditions. Consider the price of the bag, the time to pack it, rejects and rework, and any changes in cartons or freight. The better stock bag is the one that meets the order and works across those steps.
