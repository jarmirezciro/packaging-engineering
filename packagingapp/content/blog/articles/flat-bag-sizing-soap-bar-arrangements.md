Flexible packaging can be an attractive alternative to traditional cartons and rigid packaging. Bags are lightweight, take up little space before use and can reduce packaging material consumption in suitable applications. For manufacturers and logistics operations, these advantages can translate into lower packaging costs and simpler storage and handling.

But selecting the right bag is not always straightforward. A supplier typically specifies a flat bag using its length and width, while the products inside occupy three-dimensional space. Their orientation, quantity and arrangement determine how much film is needed.

Consider something as ordinary as a soap bar. Even packing twelve identical bars can produce dozens of possible bag designs. Which alternative makes the most sense for the business?

## Two flat dimensions, one three-dimensional product

A lay-flat bag is measured empty. When it opens, its two panels spread apart to wrap the product. Some of the bag’s width goes around the thickness of the bar. The bag also needs length to reach around the bottom of the product and leave room for the seal. Turn the product and you change the surfaces the film has to cover.

Both cases use the same **90 × 60 × 25 mm** soap bar, weighing **100 g**, with a 2 mm fit tolerance and a 10 mm sealing allowance.

**Technical note.** Product rotations are allowed, with no added allowance on the bar dimensions. Both cases use 1.2 mil (about 30 µm) low-density polyethylene (LDPE) at 28.04 g/m². Nominal area is the length × width of one flat face. The film weight estimate includes both walls. Twelve bars have a net weight of **1,200 g**.

## One soap bar, three bag designs

With one bar, the decision is which way to insert it. The three orientations give nominal bag areas from **110.49 cm² to 147.44 cm²**. The smallest uses about 25% less area than the largest under the same allowances. The first case shows how much the choice of insertion direction can change the film requirement before stacking even enters the picture.

## Twelve soap bars, 53 alternatives

Twelve identical soap bars, a fixed quantity per bag, and the same packaging material. It sounds like a fairly simple packaging decision.

Yet KolliPack identified 53 distinct retained design alternatives after accounting for equivalent configurations. Some arrangements required long, narrow bags; others produced wider bags or different filled-bundle shapes.

A packaging team could evaluate a few alternatives manually. The difficulty is exploring a sufficiently broad range of possibilities, comparing them consistently and repeating the exercise for different products. This is where packaging calculation software becomes useful.

Interestingly, two different product arrangements require exactly the same 252 × 212 mm flat bag. Although their packaging material requirements are identical, the resulting filled packages have different shapes. This distinction could influence subsequent packaging, handling and storage decisions. It is another reason why evaluating material consumption alone may not be sufficient.

## Where the smallest bag might win or lose

For the twelve-soap-bar example, the material difference between the first- and third-ranked designs is just 0.046 g per bag. Whether that saving is commercially meaningful depends on production volumes and what happens after the bag is filled.

Compare the lowest-material design with alternatives that give the filled package a meaningfully different shape. Check how easily each can be packed and sealed, how many fit into the shipping carton, and how those cartons perform in storage and transportation.

The soap-bar cases cover bag design and estimated film weight. A separate case shows how to evaluate the shipping carton, pallet and container. [KolliPack Packaging Flow case study](/blog/think-beyond-the-box-packaging-flow-case-study/)

## Compare the packaging that gets the soap there

Compare alternatives using the same functional unit, such as delivering 1,000 undamaged soap bars to the same destination. Count the film and secondary packaging used, the space needed for storage and transport, and any product losses. Using less film can reduce material-related impacts when the alternatives provide equivalent protection and distribution performance. [UNEP’s life cycle assessment review for bags](https://wedocs.unep.org/items/91b87abe-e0b7-4ef8-bd4a-79de18caa605)

Conventional LDPE is commonly fossil-derived. A mono-material PE bag can be a candidate if it meets sealing and protection requirements. End-of-life performance depends on local collection and sorting, and on whether inks, adhesives and closures are compatible with the recycling process. [CEFLEX guidance on flexible-packaging design choices](https://guidelines.ceflex.eu/guidelines/design-choices/)

Material efficiency is a useful starting point, but the final packaging decision should also consider manufacturing requirements, logistics efficiency, overall costs and environmental performance.
